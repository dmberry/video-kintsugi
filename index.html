Video Kintsugi

FFmpeg needed

Video Kintsugi uses FFmpeg to read, join and repair recordings, and FFmpeg isn't installed on this Mac.

Homebrew is installed, so one command does it. Install in Terminal opens Terminal and runs it; it takes a few minutes.


      

    

    

      
The simplest way is with Homebrew, the package manager for macOS. Open Terminal (Applications › Utilities) and paste these in, one after the other. The first asks for your password and may also install Apple's command line tools.

      
/bin/bash -c "$(curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Homebrew/install/HEAD/install.sh)"

      

      

      

      
FFmpeg from MacPorts (sudo port install ffmpeg) also works.

        How Video Kintsugi works
        
  
        Add one recording to fix its sound, or several to join them in the order listed. When the files share a video format (as two parts of the same recording do), the video is copied without re-encoding, so a long lecture takes a minute or two. Files that differ are re-encoded to match the first.
        
  
        The sound goes through the filter chain in step 2, shown as a mixing desk: each channel strip is one ffmpeg audio filter, worked in order from left to right. The light switches a strip on or off; knobs turn by dragging up or down, scrolling or the arrow keys, and a double-click resets one. The Text view shows the same chain as ffmpeg reads it. The presets are starting points: adjust them, make a short preview to compare before and after (it updates as you turn), and save what works as a preset of your own.
        
  
        The result is written beside the first recording unless you choose a folder, and never replaces an existing file. The originals are not changed.
        
  
        
        


  
  
        Video Kintsugi 
        
  
        Joins recordings and fixes their sound.
        
  
        Kintsugi (金継ぎ, “golden joinery”) is the Japanese craft of repairing broken pottery with urushi lacquer mixed or dusted with powdered gold. A story often told about its origin concerns the shogun Ashikaga Yoshimasa, who in the fifteenth century is said to have sent a cracked Chinese tea bowl away for repair and received it back held together with metal staples, which led craftsmen to look for a finer method.
        
  
        The repair is left in view. The gold follows the line of each break, so the object's history, including its damage, remains part of how it looks. The practice is often linked to wabi-sabi, an aesthetic of the imperfect and impermanent, and to mottainai, regret at waste.
        
  
        Video Kintsugi applies the idea to recordings: it joins a lecture saved in several parts and repairs its sound, so that a flawed recording can still be used. The gold seam in the logo marks the join.
